Method

From microfilm to cited evidence

The Time Capsule is a small retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system built for historical sources. The retrieval half runs in your browser. The generation half is optional: by default the "answer" is a set of verbatim, dated, cited quotations, and the Python toolkit can add a written summary from Claude that must cite those same passages.

1 · HarvestFind pages for each event and date range through the Library of Congress and Internet Archive APIs.
2 · OCRRead ALTO XML (word boxes and confidence), the Archive's OCR text, or run Tesseract on your own scans.
3 · CleanRejoin hyphenated words, fix long s and ligatures, drop ornament noise, score OCR quality.
4 · ChunkCut pages into ~150-word passages along printed blocks, so each keeps its box on the page.
5 · RetrieveBM25 with OCR-misreading expansion, a quality prior and per-newspaper diversity.
6 · AnswerQuote the best sentence per passage, show coverage over time, attach context; optionally write a cited summary.

Why OCR needs special care

Most digitized newspapers were scanned from decades-old microfilm and read by OCR software that struggles with faded ink, narrow columns and period type. Character accuracy on 19th-century pages can be well below 90%, so "Titanic" may appear as "Tltanic" or "Titanio". A search engine that only matches exact words silently misses that evidence. The Time Capsule handles this in three ways:

  • OCR-aware query expansion. Each search word is expanded to the misreadings an OCR engine is likely to produce (rn↔m, li↔h, cl↔d, e↔c and single-letter slips) that actually occur in the archive. A variant is only used if it is much rarer than the word itself, so real words such as "reward" are not mistaken for misreadings of "Seward". Variants count for half as much as the word you typed.
  • A quality prior. Every passage gets a 0–1 quality score from its share of letters, plausible word shapes and common English words. Noisy passages rank a little lower but never disappear, and the interface flags them.
  • The scan is one click away. For Chronicling America pages, ALTO coordinates let us request just the printed region of a passage from the Library's IIIF image server, so you can check the OCR against the page.

Why not just ask a chatbot?

A language model on its own answers from memory and can invent quotations, dates and newspaper names. Here, the evidence is always retrieved first and shown verbatim, and the optional written answer is instructed to use only those numbered passages, cite every claim, report what papers said rather than what happened, and point out disagreement between sources. You can always check the claim against the quote and the quote against the scan.

Retrieval details

  • Tokenization: lowercase, Latin accents and long s folded, stopwords removed, plural-only stemming (Harman's S-stemmer) so names are not mangled. Indic scripts (Devanagari, Bengali, Gurmukhi, Gujarati, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam) and Urdu are tokenized too, with their vowel signs kept, so Hindi passages in the Gazette of India can be searched in Hindi. OCR-misreading expansion applies to Latin-script words only.
  • Ranking: BM25 (k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75), boosted for passages that match more of the question's words, times a quality prior of 0.6 + 0.4 × quality.
  • Diversity: at most two passages per page and four per newspaper in the top results.
  • Optional dense retrieval (Python only): a small sentence-embedding model fused with BM25 by reciprocal-rank fusion, for paraphrased questions.
  • The browser and Python implementations are kept identical; the Python tests pin the shared behaviour.

Reading historical newspapers critically

  • First reports are provisional. Casualty counts, names and causes changed daily. Follow a story across dates before drawing conclusions. The Titanic, Wright brothers and armistice stories were all famously misreported at first.
  • Wire services and reprints. Small papers often printed the same Associated Press or United Press story word for word, sometimes with local cuts. Ten papers agreeing may be one source repeated.
  • Datelines vs. publication dates. A story dated "New York, April 15" may appear in a weekly printed days later. The date shown here is the issue date.
  • Partisanship and audience. Nineteenth-century papers were openly tied to parties, and most were written by and for white, middle-class, male readers. Notice whose voices are missing.
  • Offensive language. Period coverage uses racist and demeaning terms. They are left as printed for accuracy.
  • Selection bias in digitization. What was microfilmed, survived and was chosen for digitization is not a random sample of what was printed. Absence in this archive is not evidence of absence.
  • Advertising and boilerplate. Patent-medicine ads, serialized fiction and syndicated filler share the page with news, and OCR often runs columns together.

Running it yourself

The Python toolkit harvests sources, OCRs your own scans, rebuilds this site's data and answers questions from the command line, optionally writing summaries with Claude.

git clone https://github.com/ERo2203/Time-Capsule-A-News-Archive
cd Time-Capsule-A-News-Archive
pip install -e .

timecapsule ask "How did papers report the Carpathia's arrival?" --collection titanic
timecapsule harvest --only titanic --loc-pages 30     # pull more pages
timecapsule ocr ~/scans/my-paper --collection my-town  # needs: pip install -e ".[ocr]"
timecapsule build                                      # re-chunk and export site/data
timecapsule ask "..." --claude                         # needs: pip install -e ".[llm]" and an API key

To add an event, append an entry to corpus/collections.json with a date range, a Chronicling America query, a few terms the Internet Archive issues must mention, and a context note. Then run harvest and build.

Limitations

  • The archive is a curated sample of a few hundred pages and issues, not a complete record. For exhaustive work, search the source archives directly.
  • Internet Archive passages can be cited only to the issue, not the page.
  • To keep the website fast, it includes Internet Archive passages only near mentions of each event, and leaves out passages with very poor OCR. The Python toolkit searches the full text.
  • The quality score is a heuristic for ranking and warnings, not a measured character accuracy.
  • The extractive brief picks sentences by word overlap; it can miss an answer phrased differently.
